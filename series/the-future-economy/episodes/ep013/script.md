# Episode 13: "The Empty Way Back"
## Season 1 | The Future Economy

*Kirin arrives home. Dez comes in on her own terms. Hasse has been holding a story for forty-three years.*

---

The CapEx Board's eastern intake desk opened at seven, which was forty minutes after the morning rail from Kesmark arrived and thirty minutes before the city's administrative day organized itself into anything useful.

Dez had been standing at the intake desk for twenty-two minutes.

She had arrived at the transit hub from the Larch junction in a vehicle she had borrowed from the corridor network's northwest cache, which operated on an authorization system she understood in detail because she had been working within it for eight years. People who worked within systems for eight years either learned the authorization system or they stopped working. The transit clearance had been fine. What the transit clearance had not covered was the form she was currently holding.

"There's a category," she said. "External technical consultant, non-credentialed. Free Zone registered businesses can file external assessments in that category."

The desk clerk was young — twenty-three, maybe twenty-four — with the patience of someone three months into a role they were going to grow into. He was not being difficult. He was following the form.

"The Free Zone business registration needs to be in the Trade Board's current registry," he said. "We verify against the eastern Trade Board's linked list."

"My registration expired fourteen months ago." She had known this before she walked in. "Eight years in the corridor. My Free Zone credentials are in the linked list through 2124. The gap between 2124 and today is a registration maintenance issue, not a credential issue."

He looked at the form. "I can flag it for manual review. Three to five business days."

She had heard that phrase twice already this morning. The Compact administered at one speed.

"The assessment I'm filing documents a junction failure that happened two days ago," she said. She kept her voice at the register of a logistical fact, not a complaint. "The junction serves seven hundred people directly and is part of a distribution network reaching one to two million in the sector." She paused. "Does the review queue flag time-sensitive infrastructure assessments differently from standard technical submissions."

He checked. She had not expected him to check. She recalibrated her estimate of him.

"There's a flag option," he said. "Infrastructure emergency. Requires signatory from a Level Two or higher Board member authorizing the flag."

"Where do I find that person."

He looked at his terminal. "Ms. Aelfgar on the third floor is senior assessor this morning, but her calendar shows her in an incoming documentation review. She's not taking drop-ins until — "

"I'll wait," Dez said.

He looked at her. A woman in her forties, corridor-practical clothing, a case that wasn't a briefcase. Not the Board's usual morning traffic.

"Waiting area is through the glass doors," he said.

She went through the glass doors and sat in one of the functional chairs — the kind that were comfortable enough to wait in and uncomfortable enough to discourage extended use — and set her case on the chair beside her.

The information display on the wall cycled Compact administrative news without particular urgency. Guild certification updates. Commons Council budget allocations. Eastern region CapEx matching statistics. A normal morning in a normal administrative city. She had been in cities like this before. Free Zone cities with different aesthetics and the same administrative metabolism. Different forms. The same wait.

Outside, through the waiting area window, a garden bed along the building's south face. Kale, some early lettuce. A woman in a grey Commons jacket on her knees in the mud, working steadily.

She had been doing what she was doing before Dez arrived and would continue doing it after. That was the thing about the work that actually maintained things. It did not wait for the right documentation.

Dez waited.

* * *

The rail from Kesmark arrived twelve minutes late, which the departure board noted without apology in the particular way of a system that had stopped apologizing for small variances because the variances were structural rather than exceptional.

Berin stepped off the rail car beside her. He looked at the station the way he had been looking at things since the transit gate — with the recalibration of someone learning a known landscape through an altered lens. Sable River's station was what it had always been. He was registering that.

"Your registration desk first," she said.

"Yes."

The city was fully into its morning as they came out the east entrance. Guild workers moving to project sites. Commons crews doing infrastructure rounds. The particular density of a mid-sized Compact city in late morning: purposeful, calibrated, a place that had figured out its relationship to the population it currently had and was not straining. Green in the window boxes along the administrative district's main face. The spring beds going in at the edges of the commons garden she had cut through on the way to work for three years.

She had walked these streets for seven years. She was walking them differently now. Not better. Not worse. Differently.

"Find me when you're through the transit office," she said. "I'll have my comm on the city frequency."

He went toward the transit authority building. She went east.

* * *

Tomas's workshop was on the fourth block of the Guild district's eastern arm, in a converted pre-Compact commercial space that had been reworked twice and was now as close to what it wanted to be as it was going to get. Five workshops on the lower two floors, all Guild-licensed, all producing the specific percussion of skilled work through their walls — the particular sound of different materials being moved and shaped and joined by people who had been doing it long enough to do it without stopping to think about doing it.

She had been coming to this building since she was sixteen.

The door was unlocked. She knocked and went in.

He was at the bench. He was always at the bench when he wasn't somewhere else — positioned for the south window light, the surface clear, a cabinet panel in a dry fit. He was holding it at an angle that let him read the grain's cooperation with the joint. He set it down when she came in.

He looked at her without moving. The specific look of a person who had been waiting to perform an assessment and had now been given the opportunity.

"You're here," he said. Not a question.

"I called from Kesmark yesterday."

"I know." He came around the bench. "You look different."

"I've been walking for two months."

"Not that." He came closer. He was three centimeters taller than she remembered or she was remembering him from a different angle. "Different weight. In a specific way."

He moved to the side bench and filled the small electric kettle he kept there because the workshop's kitchen was two floors up and he had decided, years ago, that the walk was not worth it. He filled it without asking.

She sat on the work stool at the end of the bench. The smell of the workshop — wood and finishing oil and the fine grain dust that settled on everything and could not be entirely cleaned. She had spent years in rooms with this smell. She was noticing it now in the way you noticed things you had been filtering out.

"My grandmother," she said. "Did you know her before she was our grandmother."

He was watching the kettle.

"Say what you mean," he said.

"Kesmark. 2118. You saw her at the transit post and you didn't approach."

The room was quiet. A thump from the workshop above — someone moving material.

"She told you," he said.

"She wrote it. In a log she kept from 2091 to 2126." Kirin looked at the cabinet panel on the bench, the specific quality of old-growth grain that the Guild had made it worth her friend's while to work with carefully. "She wrote that she saw you and you saw her and you both knew not to acknowledge it. And that you'd known, since your mother came through in 2094, what the corridor was and what it was for."

Tomas set two mugs on the bench and poured when the kettle reached temperature. He never waited for a full boil. He had maintained for twenty years that the right temperature extracted what the leaves were meant to give without burning what they weren't, and Kirin had stopped arguing with it sometime around age twenty-two.

He held his mug without drinking from it.

"My mother went east in 2094," he said. "She said she was going to a coast settlement for a construction assessment. The coast settlement was ten days there and back by rail. She came back in eleven." He paused. "I was eleven years old. I didn't ask. I just knew the eleven days were different from her other travel. She came back looking at things from the inside out for a few days, and then the look faded, and she was the same as she was. But I remembered it."

The finish grinder started upstairs — the high whine of surface work on something large.

"When I was twenty-two," Tomas said, "I did an apprenticeship project at Kesmark. Transit hub renovation. Guild assignment, standard post-certification work. I was there six weeks." He looked at his mug. "The second week I saw your grandmother on the eastern platform. I recognized her from your apartment's photographs. She was with two people I didn't know, going through a door I had been walking past for two weeks and had thought was maintenance access." He paused. "I knew, from her posture and from those two people and from the door itself, that this was not ordinary. And that if she saw me acknowledging her, something ordinary would end."

"So you didn't."

"I turned around," he said. "I went back to my work. That evening, she found me in the workshop wing. She had looked for me. She said: I know you saw me. I know you know enough not to ask. That is the right amount to know." He looked at the mug. "And then she said: build things that last. Which was all she said. And I thought about it for eight years."

The grinder upstairs stopped. Then started again.

"Someone from the Board came to your workshop," she said.

"Yesterday morning." He shifted his weight, standing at the bench rather than sitting. "Compliance inquiry. She said you were filing assessments in connection with unauthorized external protocol references." The flat register of a person reciting exactly what was said to them. "I told her I hadn't heard from you in two months, which was true at the time."

"She came back."

"This morning." He paused. "She knew about the call from Kesmark. The transit hub's public comm records had apparently been checked overnight. She wanted to know what you said."

"What did you tell her."

"That you said you were coming home in two days." He met her eyes. "Which is what you said."

"And."

"And I told her I expected you this morning." His voice had gone to something controlled — not afraid, something more settled than afraid. "Because I expected you this morning. Because that's what you told me. Because I don't know how to lie well and I wasn't going to try yesterday." He paused. "She went back to wherever she came from. She said they'd follow up."

Fourteen years. Tomas at sixteen, showing her the specific grain angle at which wood cooperated and the angle at which it didn't — not as instruction, as conversation, the way he discussed everything he cared about. Tomas at twenty-two, building the bookcase for her grandmother's apartment, complaining about the irregular floor with the focused patience of someone who was going to solve the irregular floor and would rather be working than complaining. Tomas at twenty-five at the transit post at Kesmark, seeing Adaeze and not approaching, and carrying that knowledge for eight years without once saying it aloud to the person who ate dinner across from him every other week.

Eight years of knowing. The weight Adaeze's letter had described.

"Are you safe," she said. "Not safe from the compliance inquiry, specifically. Safe."

He considered this with the concreteness of a practical person assessing a practical question. "Safe as I was before you left," he said. "They've only asked questions so far." He looked at the bench. "Which doesn't mean they won't do more."

"No," she said. "It doesn't."

She held the mug. The tea was the right temperature — warm without being hot, exactly what the kettle produced at that stage. "My grandmother's letter," she said. "She wrote about you. She said the weight was too much for one person to carry alone. That it needed to be put down."

He looked at his mug.

"She was right," he said. Quiet. "It was too much. I never told her that."

"She knew."

He looked at her. Something shifted in his face — not grief exactly. Something more complicated than grief. The specific quality of a person who has been braced against something for a long time and has just been told they can stop.

"Tell me," he said. "When you're ready. Everything." He looked at the cabinet panel on the bench, the grain waiting for the joint it was going to live in for a hundred years. "I've been carrying a partial account for twenty-two years. I would like to hear the rest of it."

"Tonight," she said. "Come to my flat. Seven o'clock. I'll tell you all of it."

"I'll be there."

She finished the tea and picked up her pack. Outside, through the south window, the Guild district's midmorning going about its ordinary work. The specific sound of people who knew what they were doing, doing it.

She went out.


* * *

The transit authority's processing clerk was thorough in the way of someone who had trained for accuracy because the record mattered, and because the record would still be there after she had moved on to something else.

She took Berin's documents and read them in order — the February 2125 exit notation, the corridor transit form, the return confirmation from the transit gate — and arrived at the duration field.

"Four hundred and sixty-three days," she said.

"Yes."

She looked at the number. Then she wrote it.

"Purpose of external transit."

"Research and documentation. Outside infrastructure survey." He had prepared this phrase on the rail. "The affiliation is in my credentials record — Board-adjacent archivist before the transit. Inactive since February of last year."

She pulled the record. She read it. She pulled it again to make sure.

"The reinstatement request routes to the credentialing office," she said. "Third floor, east wing. The transit authority's flag comes off when I process this." She stamped the form. "Attach the transit confirmation to the reinstatement request. Two to three days from filing."

She printed the confirmation and set it on the desk.

He went to the third floor.

The credentialing office was staffed by two people, one of whom was on a call and one of whom was processing two things at once and absorbed a third without breaking rhythm. She took his documentation, entered the reinstatement filing, and gave him a queue confirmation. Accurate, efficient, the Compact's administrative machine doing what it did.

He came out of the Board building and stood on the pavement.

Sable River's morning was in full motion. Guild workers crossing to project sites. Commons crews doing infrastructure rounds. A delivery vehicle completing the last of its morning run. The texture of a mid-sized Compact city in late morning — purposeful, calibrated, doing the work of a population it had figured out how to accommodate without strain.

He had been outside the Compact for fifteen months.

The city was not as he remembered it. It was exactly as he remembered it. Both of these things were true, which told him something about where the difference was. He was seeing Sable River with a lens that was now calibrated for what the Outside looked like. The contrast was not dramatic — it was a city doing its normal work. But the maintenance infrastructure visible in the building faces, the commons gardens at half the block corners, the relay towers at their prescribed intervals outside the city's eastern approach: he was looking at all of it through the knowledge of what was on the other side.

He found a bench on the pavement outside the Board building's east entrance and sat on it.

Two to three days for the credentials. He had a transit confirmation and a queue number. He had no active Board status. Whatever contribution he could make was through presence — being a person a person could talk to, rather than a filing in a queue.

A sparrow landed on the bench beside him and immediately left when it determined he had nothing to offer.

He looked at the time on the transit confirmation form. She had said find her when he was through the transit office. He was through the transit office.

He picked up his pack and looked for the city frequency on his comm.

* * *

Ardent received the transit authority notification at 0840.

Berin Kosta. Corridor return clearance filed. Board reinstatement request in queue. Fourteen months of inactive status, now under review. Two to three business days.

He added it to the routing board.

The bearer's comm contact had been logged at 0713 — Tomas Verek, Guild carpenter, workshop in the Guild district's eastern arm. The compliance inquiry had run two visits: yesterday's initial and this morning's follow-up. The follow-up had confirmed the arrival and disclosed an expected contact with Brigid Aelfgar at the CapEx Board, possibly this morning or tomorrow.

He looked at the routing board.

The Meridian courier envelope had been delivered to Brigid Aelfgar's registry at 0830 the previous day. He had the delivery timestamp from the courier service's automated tracking. He did not have the contents of the envelope. He had the fact of delivery, the origin account from the tracking record, and the established connection between the origin account and the Outside relay.

The materials assessment was in the review queue: fourteen submissions ahead of it, reviewer processing three to five per day.

The bearer was in Sable River. The second carrier had filed a reinstatement request with an inactive credential. An unaffiliated outside party — he had the transit clearance from the perimeter system — had come through a different route, filing infrastructure documentation through the Board's external consultant intake. Manual review pending. He did not know who this party was.

Three people carrying documentation he could not intercept.

He looked at the routing board for a long time.

His apparatus had two operational modes. The first was what he had used for eighteen years: documentary. Monitor the chain, watch the relays, apply administrative friction where possible, avoid direct contact with individuals inside the Compact's own system. The mode left no record of the kind that a future review board would find operational. He was a person who managed information, not people. His authority was narrow, specific, derived from a mandate that had been documented once in 2108 and renewed three times since without significant modification.

The second mode was what he had never used: presence. Having someone physically watch the bearer in the city. Note her contacts. Report her movements. The kind of surveillance his apparatus applied routinely outside the Compact's boundaries — in the corridor, near the transit gate — but had never applied inside Sable River.

To a Compact citizen on Compact soil.

He opened the contact list for Sable River. Two administrative contacts — people who handled local filing logistics, maintained his information flow through the Board's system. They were not field operatives. They were people who answered questions and filed forms. He could ask one of them to watch the bearer's movements through the city.

He sat with the question for a long time.

Eighteen years. He had managed this from inside the Compact's administrative framework because the framework was the thing that made the management possible. Not legal — his mandate was specific, his authority narrow, his operations built around the specific gaps in the Compact's own oversight structure. But structural. He had never stepped outside the structure. He had watched. He had documented. He had applied administrative friction. He had done nothing that could not be described, under review, as the normal function of an internal security operation within its defined parameters.

The moment he asked someone to follow a Compact citizen through her own city, he was in different territory.

He thought about Adaeze Okonkwo.

He had been tracking the Okonkwo-line for twelve years. He had monitored her building, reviewed her filings, maintained the watch flag on her address of record. She had filed her annual assessment reports through the eastern district queue. She had tended her garden. She had worked her shifts at the elder-care facility and come home and made her tea and folded her linens.

She had died in January 2126 of cardiac failure. Advanced, consistent with her age, consistent with two years of declining medical records. The medical record had nothing operational in it.

He had not harmed her. He had watched her for twelve years and she had lived and worked and died on her own terms, in her apartment, in her city, in the Compact she had served as a material assessor and compliance filer for thirty-five years. He had found the key because her granddaughter had found the box. Not because of anything he had done.

He had not harmed her.

He also had not fixed the junctions. He had known about the junctions for twelve years, and he had held that knowledge because fixing the junctions required explaining why he had the data, and explaining why he had the data required explaining the systems he monitored, and explaining those systems required explaining what was at the other end of the sensor networks.

He had been managing the acknowledgment of the Outside. Not its reality.

The junctions had been failing whether the Compact acknowledged the Outside or not.

He closed the contact list without selecting anyone.

He would not ask someone to follow the bearer through Sable River. Not because he lacked the resources. Because once he did it, once his apparatus applied physical surveillance to a Compact citizen on Compact soil, he was no longer the apparatus he had built. He would be something else. And the thing he had built had held for eighteen years precisely because it had known what it was.

The bearer would go to Brigid Aelfgar. The documentation was in three channels and none of them were reachable without becoming visible in ways that changed what he was.

He could hold his line and lose, or cross it and become something else.

He closed the routing board.

He went to get coffee. It had been cold for an hour. He poured it out and made a fresh cup and sat at the desk in the room without windows and looked at the wall.

He had been keeping a door closed for eighteen years. The room on the other side had been filling with water for twenty-seven. The person he had flagged had filed a maintenance report that a reviewer in his own system would eventually open.

He could not stop any of it.

He would have to think, very carefully, about what he was managing toward and whether that thing was still what he thought it was.

* * *

She found Dez in the waiting area of the Board's eastern intake at half past one.

She had come to the Board for Hasse, but Hasse was not the thing in the waiting area. Dez was in the waiting area, in one of the functional chairs, her case on the chair beside her, doing the patient thing that corridor work had made her specifically good at.

Dez looked up when Kirin came through the glass door.

"You're late," she said. Not an accusation. An observation about the rail schedule.

"Twelve minutes." Kirin sat. "How long have you been here."

"Since seven-fifteen." Dez looked at the glass door to the intake desk. "I have infrastructure documentation from an independent source. Corridor operator, eight years, independent monitoring data on the sector six junction and the adjacent network. Compiled from my own monitoring and from my contact's projection data, separate from the Outside network's formal relay chain." She paused. "The filing category for this is External Technical Consultant. My Free Zone credentials expired fourteen months ago. Manual review, three to five days."

"Which is too slow."

"Which is too slow." Dez looked at the desk through the glass. "Ms. Aelfgar on the third floor has been in a documentation review since this morning. She hasn't come out. I haven't been able to reach the signatory process."

Kirin looked at the ceiling. Brigid in a documentation review on the same morning a Meridian courier envelope had arrived with a junction list and a Rootstock Protocol active status from Dara.

"She's reading your documentation right now," Kirin said. "Not yours specifically. But what you're trying to tell her."

Dez looked at her.

"The broker in Meridian held the population atlas for eleven years," Kirin said. "When the relay came from the second way-station, she put a junction list in an envelope she'd had ready for eight months, addressed it to Brigid Aelfgar's registry, and put it in the morning courier stack. It arrived yesterday morning." She paused. "Junction list, protocol status, the broker's authentication. Brigid's been waiting since 2122. She's reading right now."

Dez sat with this for a moment. "Then she knows the junction failed."

"She will. By the time she finishes the envelope."

Dez looked at her hands — the hands of someone who had been doing practical outdoor work for eight years. "I came through the corridor on my own route," she said. "My infrastructure contact gave me updated projections. The sector six failure was in the projections as four to seven days from the contact date. When I calculated the transit time to Sable River, it was going to be close." She paused. "I came anyway."

"Why."

Dez looked at the information display cycling the wall. "Because I've been watching the eastern sector water management for two years from my own position in the corridor. Because the projections have been accurate. And because — " she stopped. "I've been in the corridor for eight years. I know what happens to outside documentation when it goes into a system staffed for a different map."

"It doesn't get read."

"It doesn't get read." She looked at Kirin. "I wanted to be here in person when this documentation came in. To say: I have independent data from a different source. Not the Outside network. Not a CapEx Board employee. A corridor operator who has been watching the water management independently for two years. That is a third corroboration, from a different origin, with a different methodology." She paused. "Does it matter."

"It matters," Kirin said. "It matters that the documentation has more than one origin. It matters that you're a person Brigid can talk to, not a filing in a queue."

Dez held that. Not relieved — she was not the kind of person who showed relief. She looked like someone who had made a calculation and was watching the results come in. "The Meridian contact also told me," she said, "that the firm looking for Rootstock-compatible hardware found a different authentication avenue. The procurement inquiry is still in the Free Zone registry."

"I know."

"The other key." She paused. "Whatever they found, it's not a physical token that came through the standard corridor route. I've been in the managed sector for eight years. I would have heard about a Rootstock-compatible token moving through it." She looked at the glass door. "Whatever avenue they found, it's a different route than the one I can see."

Kirin filed this. She did not know yet what it changed.

"Tomorrow morning," she said. "I have a meeting with Brigid. If you want to present your documentation in person — I'll get you into that meeting."

Dez looked at her.

"Why."

"Because you're here. Because you came." Kirin looked at the glass door. "Because corroboration from a different source is the difference between one person's account and a pattern. And because you've done good work, for your own reasons, in your own way." She stood. "Tomorrow. Nine o'clock. Third floor."

Dez held her look for a moment. Not warm. The look of a person who was filing what had been said under the category of things she would act on.

"Nine o'clock," she said.


* * *

She found Hasse not at a filing terminal but at a worktable in the materials archive on the ground floor of the Board's west wing, with three pre-Compact binders open in front of him and a loupe he had set down at some point and not picked up again. He looked up when she came through the archive door.

He did not look surprised. She had the sense, looking at him, of someone who had been ready for this conversation without knowing exactly when it would arrive.

"Kirin," he said.

"Hasse."

She sat across from him. The archive smell — preservation compound and old paper and the particular quality of material that had been kept well. She had been in and out of this room for three years. She had thought it was the work she liked; now she thought it was the smell, which was the smell of things held carefully over time.

She set the key on the table between them.

He looked at it for a long time without touching it.

"You found what you were looking for," he said.

"Yes."

He looked at her. "Are you safe."

"For now." She watched him look at the key. "I want to know about the one you saw before."

He sat back. He looked at the binders in front of him in the way of a person using a fixed point in the visual field while they organized the order of words.

"2083," he said. "I had been at the Board for eighteen years. The pre-Compact materials record reconciliation — the first systematic cross-reference between the Board's instrument documentation and the Archive's holdings. Nobody had tried to make the two systems talk to each other. It was the kind of project you give to someone competent who doesn't need to make friends while doing it."

"I know the project," she said. "You handed me the first three sections when I started."

"Yes." He paused. "A person came to the Board. Walk-in. Said they had a material they needed evaluated. The intake clerk sent them to me because I was the pre-Compact specialist and the object looked old." He paused. "It was a key. Same protocol family as yours. Different form factor — flatter, slightly longer, the etching pattern running horizontally rather than in the diagonal grid yours has. Same material composition. Same warmth when held."

"Continuous warmth."

"Even sitting on the table." He looked at the key between them. "You know what that means now."

"Active authentication circuit. Hardware token in continuous baseline handshake state."

"I didn't know that in 2083. I ran the standard assays and got what you got — the database doesn't go back far enough. The material predates the Ledger." He paused. "I told the person: I can't tell you what this is from here. Go to the Territorial Archive. The archivist there — Perun had been in that building for twelve years by then — he'll have the pre-Compact protocol documentation. He'll know this system."

"What did they say."

"That they needed to be somewhere else for a day and would come back for the object." He looked at the table. "They left it with me as security. Or trust. I waited a week. Then two months." He looked at her. "I took the object to Perun and told him the story."

"And Perun recognized it."

"Immediately. He had been looking for a physical instance of the Rootstock Protocol's hardware for six years. The Archive had documentation — fragments, mostly, in the pre-Compact legal materials section — but no physical token. He took it and told me: the person will either come back, or they won't. If they come back, I'll have found more by then. If they don't — " he looked at Kirin — "it means something stopped them."

"They didn't come back."

"No." He was quiet for a moment. The archive's particular silence — not emptiness but containment. A room in which things were held. "I didn't know enough in 2083 to think usefully about what stopped them. I know more now. Forty-one years at this Board, watching what goes into the eastern sector's documentation queue. Watching what gets read and what doesn't." He looked at his hands. "I think someone was managing the Outside's non-existence in 2083 the same way someone has been managing it since. And I think the person with the key encountered that management in some form. I don't know if it means they were turned back, or discouraged, or — " he stopped. "I don't know. I only know they didn't come back."

"But you sent me to the Archive," she said. "The same advice you gave them in 2083."

"Yes."

"Because it was the right advice."

"Yes." He held her eyes for a moment. "And because I wanted to know if you would come back."

The archive held its silence. The afternoon light came through the high windows at its specific spring angle — the quality of light that existed only in the late afternoon of a Compact city in April, slanted and amber and without urgency.

"Perun still has the third token," she said.

He nodded. "I asked him when you came to me in February. He confirmed it's in the archive holdings, catalogued as unattributed pre-Compact authentication hardware." He paused. "He said you would find what you needed without it. That the protocol doesn't require a third token. Two witness appointments are sufficient."

"What is it."

"He described it as a founding precursor. The first physical attempt at the two-witness structure, before the appointment framework was finalized. A prototype — the protocol developers making hardware before the credential logic was settled. It doesn't carry an appointment. It's a proof of concept, not a credential." He looked at the binders. "It was in someone's possession in the corridor in 2083. A person who walked into this building and sat in this chair — the equivalent of this chair — and asked a materials analyst what they were holding."

She looked at the binders. "Those are the pre-Compact intake records."

"The 2070s and 2080s section," he said. "I've been working through it since February. I thought if someone brought a token into the Board and left it, there would be an intake record." He opened the second binder. He turned to a page marked with a folded strip of paper.

The intake log was handwritten — the Board's records from that period were still in the analog system, the digital transition having happened in the 2090s. The handwriting was neat, compressed in the way of someone who wrote a great deal and had developed efficiency. It was dated March 17, 2083.

*Walk-in evaluation request. One pre-Compact authentication token, material class undetermined. Referred to senior analyst H. Lindgren for evaluation.*

Below that, in a slightly different ink — the closing notation from when the file was finalized:

*Object transferred to Territorial Archive per analyst's recommendation. Claimant did not return. —March 19.*

She read it once. She read it again, not because she had missed something but because the forty-three years between the date on the page and the afternoon she was sitting in asked for both readings.

*Claimant did not return.*

"No name," she said.

"The pre-Compact intake process didn't require names for evaluation requests. They weren't credentialed holders. They were just people who walked in." He looked at the page. "I don't know who they were. I know what they left, and that they didn't come back for it."

She closed the binder.

She looked at Hasse — at forty-one years at the Board, at the particular kind of patience that was not patience but accumulated attention. The quality of someone who had been doing their work carefully for a very long time without knowing whether the care would eventually matter, and who had continued doing it anyway because that was the kind of person they were.

"Tomorrow morning," she said. "I go to Brigid. I want to tell you what I found — all of it — after that conversation. In detail."

He held her eyes.

"I'll be here," he said. "I usually am."

She went out of the archive and through the Board's main corridor and out into the late afternoon.

* * *

Berin was on the pavement bench where she had last seen him. His pack between his feet. He looked up when she came out.

"Credentials," she said.

"Queue confirmation. Two to three days." He paused. "I cannot file anything through the Board system until the reinstatement clears."

"You don't need to file." She sat beside him. "The archive documentation is in four channels and you're not needed in any of them." She paused. "What you're needed for is to be a person who can talk to Brigid about what's in the archive. In a meeting. Tomorrow morning." She looked at the Board building. "Nine o'clock. Third floor. You, me, a corridor operator named Dez who has independent infrastructure documentation."

He looked at the transit confirmation in his hand. "And Brigid."

"And Brigid. Who received the Meridian envelope yesterday morning and has been reading it all day."

He was quiet for a moment. He looked at the street — the Guild district's late afternoon, the specific angle of the spring light on the building faces, the quality of a city whose maintenance had been continuous. "I came back to present the archive documentation in person," he said. "That's what I said I came back to do."

"Tomorrow morning," she said. "You get to do it."

She stood.

"The commons housing office is two blocks from the Board," she said. "They process temporary residents on transit documentation. Find somewhere to stay tonight."

He picked up his pack. He did it without narrating the process — efficiently, the way he did everything, years of practice having made the efficiency automatic. "And you," he said.

"I have somewhere to be," she said.

She went north.

* * *

She stopped in the transit hub's public comm room and sent word to Tomas: tonight, still, seven o'clock, her flat. Then she walked the eight blocks north to the residential district behind the Guild quarter.

Adaeze's apartment building was mid-rise, Keeper-class masonry, pre-Compact original construction that had been maintained rather than renovated — the specific quality of a building that had been cared for rather than updated, the original bones kept, the structural integrity current. Adaeze had lived there for forty years. Before that she had lived somewhere else. Before the Compact she had been someone the Compact's records did not know.

Kirin had the building key from February. The lock had not been changed.

The apartment was the same.

Someone had been maintaining it — the window box herbs had been watered, the kitchen had been wiped down in the careful way of a person who cleaned because they were supposed to rather than because it gave them satisfaction, a card from the building association on the counter: *We are maintaining the unit as a held estate per the registry. Please contact the commons office on the second floor with questions regarding the estate status.*

She stood in the main room.

Adaeze had arranged this space in an arrangement that had not significantly changed in decades. The work table by the window, the reading chair, the kitchen with its specific shelf organization — practical things at the front, occasional things toward the back, nothing without a use and everything with a place. A room that had been doing its function for a long time without requiring attention.

She went to the kitchen window.

The garden below: four square meters of shared plot, running along the building's south face. Still producing kale from the winter bed, the spring planting beginning at the edges. And the woman from the second floor — she had been there when Kirin was a child, always in the garden at the same time, always with the same focused attention. She was there now, on her knees in the spring mud, pressing seeds in with the deliberate precision of someone who had learned the depth through repetition rather than instruction.

She had been tending it since before Adaeze died. She would tend it after.

The closet in the back hall was the same closet. She opened it.

The linens were there. Folded in Adaeze's arrangement — the heavy items at the bottom, the lighter at the top, the guest linens in the labeled section on the right, the quality of an organization that had been established once and maintained without thought. The box was not there. She had taken the box in February and the box was now wherever she was, which was her flat six blocks away.

But the shape of where the box had been was still there: the slight settling of the linens on either side, the memory of a weight that had organized this shelf for thirty-five years. Not hidden. Kept. The way you kept a thing you were responsible for until it was time for someone else to take it.

She closed the closet.

She went back to the kitchen and stood at the window looking at the garden.

There was a thing she had been carrying since Salt — since she had sat at Naila's table with the envelope Adaeze had left there in September 2125 and had opened it and read it once in the insufficient light of a place she needed to leave. She had read it again on the rail. She had read it a third time in the transit hub at Kesmark before she found Berin.

*The key has been in a closet behind thirty years of linens. You found it. That is the whole job. Whatever you do next, you have done the job.*

She had done the job.

The junction was failed. The documentation was in three channels, all of them still moving. The formatted survey was still assembling somewhere in the eastern archive network, timeline unknown. The materials assessment was in a review queue. The Meridian envelope was in Brigid's hands and Brigid had been reading it all day.

Tomorrow morning, at nine o'clock, she would go to the third floor and sit down across from Brigid Aelfgar and give her what she had carried for two and a half months across four hundred miles of managed corridor and un-Compact territory and back.

She would not tell Brigid what to do with it. Adaeze had been specific about that.

Outside: the spring evening beginning, the light on the garden at the day's last angle. The woman from the second floor finishing her work — she stood up, brushed mud from her knees, looked at what she had planted. She stood there for a moment, not with any particular expression, just looking. Then she went inside.

The garden was there. The seeds were in.

Kirin stood at the window with both keys warm in her jacket pocket — the disc and the card, the founding appointment and the interior appointment, both together in the settled continuous warmth the handshake had left them in. They had been warm since Rua's work table on the plateau. They would be warm tomorrow in Brigid's office.

She looked at the garden for a while, listening to the city outside. The specific sound of Sable River in the evening: a city that knew what it was, doing what it did, not yet knowing what was coming through the door.

She turned off the kitchen light. She went out.
