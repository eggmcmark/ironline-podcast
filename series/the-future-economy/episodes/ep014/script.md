# Episode 14: "Assessment Day"
## Season 1 | The Future Economy

*Kirin delivers what she carried. Brigid receives it. The city does not yet know what is coming through the door.*

---

Brigid Aelfgar had been at the kitchen table since twenty past four.

The Meridian envelope was spread in the order she had been developing since yesterday morning — a system, or the beginning of a system, a person making spatial sense of documentation by laying it out in the order her reading had required. The junction list on the left. The Rootstock Protocol active status notation in the center. The authentication cipher from the Meridian broker on the right.

And the photograph sleeve, which had arrived separately — not in the envelope, but through the Board's materials assessment courier, routed to her registry by the emergency infrastructure flag someone had managed to attach to the filing. She had not opened the photograph sleeve. She knew what was in it. She had known what the envelope would contain since April 3, when she had received a transit notification for a Board employee named Kirin Saleh, listed as personal leave, duration unspecified, origin point: eastern corridor approach.

She had been waiting since 2122. Four years of quiet.

The coffee was cold. She made a second cup and did not bring it back to the table. She stood at the kitchen window instead.

Her apartment was on the fourth floor of a building in the residential district north of the Guild quarter. The view from the kitchen window: a courtyard garden, the rear faces of two other buildings, the particular quality of predawn Sable River — a city that slept without apology, that had enough people to run itself and not enough to be busy at twenty past six in the morning. A maintenance bot was working along the courtyard's south edge, its motion steady and unhurried.

She had been a Foundation Assessor for nineteen years. She had made the third-tier certification in 2107 — the year the Compact's eastern territories formally completed their infrastructure consolidation, the year the last of the pre-Compact buffer zones had been written off as unproductive for Ledger purposes. She had been thirty years old. She had just finished the Board's cross-credentialing program and had her full assessment authority. She had read the consolidation documents as a matter of professional development and had noted, in the margins of her copy, a figure that did not reconcile with the assessment data she had been generating.

The figure was a population estimate. The official estimate for the depopulated eastern zone: four hundred thousand. The figure she had in the eastern sector infrastructure assessment data — buried in the maintenance logs, in the service-capacity utilization numbers, in the load assumptions the maintenance systems were running to — was seven times that.

She had not reported the discrepancy.

She had done something more useful. She had kept it. She had spent four years building documentation of the discrepancy with the care of someone who understood that documentation was most useful when it was complete and could not be dismissed as partial. Then in 2122 she had recognized something in a materials assessment that crossed her desk — a routine corrosion analysis of a water management component — and she had recognized the filing methodology. She had sent an inquiry through a Compact-adjacent channel she had reason to believe was supervised, and the channel had replied: *There is a person who will come. Be here.*

Four years.

She looked at the courtyard garden. The maintenance bot had finished its south edge pass and was starting along the east wall.

She had the question ready. She had prepared it the way she prepared every assessment question she asked, which was: know the answer before you ask, ask it to make the answer on record. The question was: *If the Compact has to acknowledge the Outside — all of it, the population, the failed junctions, the seven million people living outside the Ledger — what does that cost?*

She knew what she was going to do with the answer.

She picked up the second cup of coffee and brought it to the table and sat down.

At nine o'clock she would be in the third-floor meeting room. She had cleared her calendar, set the room to restricted access, and asked the intake desk to hold her correspondence until noon. She had done all of this yesterday afternoon, before anyone was watching her do it.

The thing about decision-making — and she had spent nineteen years making decisions that other people had thought were assessments — was that the decision happened before the meeting, in the quiet, before the words that made it official. The meeting was formality. It was the record. The decision had already been made.

She finished the coffee. The predawn city outside her window was becoming early morning, the quality of light shifting from dark to grey. The maintenance bot completed its courtyard round and returned to its bay.

She went to get dressed.

* * *

Kirin had been awake since five.

Tomas had left at half past ten the previous night — late, by the reckoning of people who worked before the light was good, which he did. She had told him everything. It had taken two hours and forty minutes, not counting the time he spent asking questions — the corridor questions first, then the archive, then the plateau and the junction designations and the Rootstock Protocol, and finally, at the end, the Ardent question, which he sat with for a long time before asking a different question than she expected.

"Does he know where you're going tomorrow," he said.

"He has the Board's calendar system. The meeting room is restricted access, which is visible if you know how to look at the Board's operational log." She paused. "So: probably."

Tomas looked at the kitchen window. "And does that change anything."

"Not for me," she said. "I don't think it changes anything for him either. He's out of options inside his current authority. If he were going to escalate, he would have done it already."

She had said it with more certainty than she felt.

This morning: the city in its April light. Her flat in the residential district behind the markets — the same flat she'd had for three years, the same view of the cross-street, the window box needing to be replanted after two months of absence. She had not replanted it. She had made tea and stood at the window and done what she had been doing since Salt: assess the actual situation. Not the situation as it should be. The situation as it was.

The Board's eastern intake desk opened at seven. She went in through the north entrance, which took her past the commons garden that supplied the staff canteen, two volunteers and a Commons crew member with a wheelbarrow of compost working in the spring beds. She had been walking past this garden for three years without looking at it with any particular attention. She looked at it now.

Inside: the Board's morning. She did not stop at her own desk — her status was still personal leave, the compliance flag active on her account. She went straight to the third floor.

* * *

The meeting room was small. A table that seated six. A window looking out on the rear garden. A display surface on the wall, switched off. The kind of room that was booked for conversations that needed a door.

Dez was already there. Her case beside her chair, the documentation she had been assembling for two years organized in the way of someone whose organizational system had been refined by a long time doing work without redundancy. Berin was on her left, transit confirmation on the table in front of him, the archive notes in the order he had established on the walk from the plateau.

Brigid came in at eight fifty-nine.

A woman in her mid-fifties — the particular quality of someone who had been doing one kind of work for long enough that the work had become visible in how they moved through a room. She looked at the three of them. She sat down. She put a folder on the table in front of her and did not open it.

"I received the Meridian envelope Monday morning," she said. "I have been reading it since Monday morning." She looked at each of them in turn. "I know why you're here."

She looked at Kirin. "I've also been reading the materials assessment — the one filed under the Compact's emergency infrastructure protocol, citing the S-6E failure and the monitoring case chain. That documentation will be in formal review by end of week." A pause. "I didn't know until I saw your filing that you were the one who submitted it."

"I used my Board credentials at Kesmark."

"You used a compliance flag that someone else applied to your account to push the assessment into senior review rather than the standard queue." The flat precision of a person describing a mechanism she recognized. "Which was either very clever or very lucky."

"I didn't know the flag would do that when I filed," Kirin said. "I knew the flag existed."

Something shifted in Brigid's face — not a smile, the near-expression of a person who had been in the assessment business long enough to recognize competent improvisation. "No," she said. "You wouldn't have."

She opened the folder. Three sheets. She laid them on the table.

The first was the junction list: the eleven designations, the condition status, the Compact monitoring case numbers, the failure date for S-6E. Rua's handwriting at the top, the plateau council's notation at the bottom, Kirin's own additions in the margin.

The second was a page Kirin did not recognize until she looked at it twice — a copy, printed on the Board's thermal document stock, of a pre-Compact equity protocol registration. The Rootstock Protocol. Filed 1978. Two witness appointments. Status: active, as of March 4, 2126.

The third page was blank.

"The third page," Brigid said, "is for what you tell me this morning that isn't in the documentation."

She looked at Kirin. The assessor's look — nineteen years of it. "Walk me through what you found. In order."

So Kirin did.

She talked for twenty-two minutes without stopping. She talked through the archive, the transit, the plateau, the council meeting. She talked through the junction list and where each number came from — who had assessed it, when, under what conditions, with what margin of error. She talked through the two keys and the joint handshake and what the Tamsat letters said about who held which appointment. She talked through Dara's account of the EQU-convertibility threat and Ardent's administrative flags and what the documentation chain looked like from the inside, from the position of someone who had been carrying it for two and a half months.

When she stopped, Brigid looked at Berin. "The archive at sector twelve. The formatted survey."

"In assembly," Berin said. "The location note I sent estimated seven to ten days from relay receipt. That was ten days ago." He paused. "I believe it's complete or close to complete. I don't have a confirmation."

Brigid looked at Dez. "The independent monitoring data."

Dez set her case on the table and laid out the documentation in the order she had organized it: two years of sector six junction monitoring, independent methodology, compiled from her position in the corridor. The projections at the top. The source data underneath. "Different origin from the Outside network's relay chain," she said. "Different instruments, different observation points, same trend. The failure was projected in my data as four to seven days from my last reading. The failure happened in that window."

Brigid looked at the documentation. She did not touch it. She looked at it the way she looked at things she was filing.

Then she asked the question.

"If the Compact has to acknowledge the Outside — all of it, the seven million people, the twenty-seven years of failed monitoring, the junctions that have been degrading on our watch without our response — what does that cost?"

The room held itself.

Kirin looked at the junction list on the table. S-6E. Failed April 12. Storage water gone forty-eight hours after failure. Growing season six weeks out. A basin agricultural operation going into its most critical period with sixty percent of its irrigation supply missing.

"I don't know," she said. "I know what the cost of not acknowledging it is. The junctions go. The sectors they serve lose water. The population adapts or doesn't, without any Compact knowledge there's something to respond to." She paused. "What it costs to acknowledge it — politically, institutionally, in terms of the Compact's self-understanding — I can't tell you. I'm a materials analyst, not an assessor at your level."

Brigid held her eyes.

"No," she said. "You're not."

She looked at all three of them. "I'm going to initiate a formal assessment under the Board's emergency infrastructure protocol. The junction failure gives me standing. The protocol allows me to extend the assessment scope to all junctions in the documentation chain, with outside-origin evidentiary support, pending the formatted survey. Three to five days." She paused. "That's what I can promise you. The assessment starts. What comes after it is not something I can promise."

Dez said: "The sector six basin has six weeks before the growing season. The replacement project is underway on the plateau, but the timeline is tight."

"I understand that." The flat precision again — not cold, the opposite of cold, a warmth so compressed it had become accuracy. "I'll contact the Commons Council's emergency infrastructure division this morning. The junction failure is already in the monitoring system. They haven't read it yet. I'll make sure they read it."

She gathered the three sheets. She looked at the junction list one more time.

"Adaeze Okonkwo," she said. "I met her once. Fourteen years ago. She came to this building. She said: there is documentation in the system, and one day a person will bring you what you need to act on it." A pause. "She didn't tell me it would be her granddaughter."

Kirin said nothing.

"She was a remarkable woman," Brigid said. "I know you know that." She looked at Kirin. "And so are you. I want you to understand that I know what you carried." She stood. "The assessment starts today."

She took the folder and went out.

The three of them sat in the small room with the door closed.

Dez said: "The emergency infrastructure division. Do they actually respond in six weeks."

"I don't know," Kirin said. "I've never tested it." She looked at the table where the junction list had been. "She initiated the assessment. That's what she said she could promise."

"It's more than we had this morning," Berin said. He said it without optimism. With the quality of a person who had spent fifteen months living inside a timeline they couldn't control and had developed a working relationship with incremental progress. "It's more than we had this morning."

Kirin stood.

"Nine o'clock," she said. "We made nine o'clock."


* * *

After the meeting Dez said she was staying in Sable River.

Kirin had not expected this.

"The Meridian contact has a question about the authentication pathway for the sector twelve archive documentation," Dez said. They were in the corridor outside the meeting room, her case in hand. "Someone needs to be available to answer it who knows both ends of the chain. I know both ends of the chain." She paused. "Also I haven't slept in a real building in eight years. The transit commons housing is acceptable."

"It is," Kirin said. "Did you file for it last night."

"At seven-fifteen with my transit documentation. They process independent consultants with expired credentials on a temporary basis." She looked at Kirin. "The filing category is still in manual review. But they gave me the room."

Kirin looked at her. The particular quality of a person who had solved problems for eight years in environments that didn't admit of unsolvable ones.

"The manual review," Kirin said. "When you see Brigid — and you will, at some point in the next few days — ask her to clear the filing flag. She has the authority."

Dez looked at her. Not quite a smile. "Yes," she said. "I know."

She went toward the stairwell. Kirin watched her go.

* * *

At eleven seventeen, the terminal in Ardent's windowless room received a notification from the Board's assessment routing system.

He had not realized until this moment that he had read access to this routing. He had maintained, for eighteen years, read-only credentials in the eastern CapEx Board's compliance audit trail — a standard internal security provision, one of the narrow operational authorities documented in the 2108 mandate and its renewals. It had never produced anything useful. The Board processed six hundred to nine hundred assessment filings per quarter. He had not thought to weight this channel.

The notification: *Emergency infrastructure protocol initiated — formal assessment opening — S-6E failure and associated network designations, S-6E through S-8N, N-4W through N-7E, eleven designations total. Assessed at request of Foundation Assessor B. Aelfgar, authorization Level Three. Documentation scope extended to outside-origin evidentiary support per Emergency Protocol 7(c). Assessment ID: 2126-0415-EIP-04. Documentation to be reviewed in full.*

He read it once. He read it again.

He opened the assessment ID in the routing system.

The documentation chain was visible in the filing record: materials assessment submitted by a Board employee with an active compliance flag — the flag he had filed — junction list from an external source, population atlas reference via Meridian Trade Board courier, corroboration filing from an unidentified external consultant. Independent monitoring data, two years, sector six water management.

Kel said from the second desk: "Want me to pull the full documentation."

"No," Ardent said.

Kel noted the response and went back to his screen.

Ardent looked at the assessment ID.

Foundation Assessor Aelfgar had emergency protocol authority under the Compact's infrastructure management framework — a provision he had not considered when he had mapped her known connections and Rootstock-adjacent history. He had modeled her as a relay point. The person the documentation was intended for. The endpoint of the chain. He had not modeled her as someone who could independently initiate a formal process that placed the entire documentation chain into the Compact's official assessment queue, under Level Three authority, in a way his apparatus had no mechanism to stop without becoming visible.

He should have. He knew her record.

This was the third time in eighteen years that his frame had been wrong.

The first time: he had looked for the Rootstock key in Adaeze Okonkwo's professional connections, her transit records, her pre-Compact financial filings. He had not looked in a closet.

The second time: he had modeled Adaeze's Sable River placement as concealment, as a strategy of hiddenness. It was not concealment. It was infrastructure. The key needed to be inside the Compact, accessible to a successor, and the simplest way to keep a thing in the Compact was to keep it there.

The third time: he had modeled the filing as a chain to disrupt, a threat to intercept. He had not modeled it as a process that, once initiated through official channels by someone with emergency assessment authority, became something his apparatus had no mechanism to stop without becoming visible in exactly the way it could not afford.

He sat with this.

The assessment was in the Board's official queue. The documentation was on record. Foundation Assessor Aelfgar had Level Three emergency authority. If his apparatus filed an objection to the assessment, the objection would require explaining the basis for the objection, which would require explaining the monitoring apparatus, which would require explaining what was at the other end of the sensor networks.

He could not stop it. Not without becoming it. Not without the apparatus he had built for eighteen years becoming, in the public record, the thing that had been suppressing evidence of a failing water management system serving millions of people.

He had been keeping a door closed. The door was open.

He thought about what he had been protecting. The 4.2 billion people inside the Compact whose lives were built on a stable system. The destabilizing effect of a legitimate Rootstock Protocol filing that the Compact would be structurally obligated to process. He thought about what "structurally obligated" meant when the thing being claimed was not assets but acknowledgment — the formal recognition that the territory had people in it, that those people had infrastructure, that the infrastructure was failing.

He thought about what it would mean if the Compact processed that acknowledgment in an orderly way, through its official assessment machinery. Rather than the kind of structural rupture that happened when a cover-up broke instead of opening.

He had been managing acknowledgment. Not reality.

He closed the assessment routing.

He opened a blank document.

He sat for a long time.

Then he began to write — not a report to his superiors, not yet. A working document. The kind of document that organized what he had known and for how long and what he had done with the knowing. A document that, if read by a future review board, would show a person who had operated within a narrow mandate and had made choices inside that mandate that he was now prepared to describe in full.

He was not going to file this document today.

He was going to be ready to file it.

That was a different thing from what he had been doing for eighteen years. He sat with the difference.

Kel said: "The S-6E monitoring flag has been logged by Commons Council emergency infrastructure. Relay sent to their eastern response team."

"Yes," Ardent said. He kept writing.

* * *

Three days after she had put the last of the Curator's operatives on the wrong road, Naila Brace received word that they had figured it out.

The relay came through the corridor network's northwest chain — obliquely, in the way sensitive information moved through a network that could not afford to be specific. The phrase was: *northern redirect trace confirmed, source identified.* Which meant: they knew it was her.

She read it once. She put the relay in the morning's paper correspondence pile.

Outside the kitchen window: the corridor highway in its morning state. An eastbound freight carrier on the service road. Two maintenance bots working on a resurfacing project near the eastern junction. One of the resident travelers tending the small cultivated patch in the forecourt — a strip of reclaimed soil where an oil-change station had been, sixty years ago, now producing enough greens to supplement four people's daily meals.

Naila had been keeping Salt for eleven years. Before that she had been a medic in the Free Zone's eastern dispatch network, working a trauma route between coastal hospitals and the outer settlements. She had come to the corridor in 2115 with a go-bag and a maintenance toolkit and a working knowledge of what a body needed to stay alive when the supply chain was unreliable. She had built the way-station from a shell of a structure because the corridor needed one and no one else had done it.

The network would come to her now. Not with hostility — they were not in the business of hostility. With documentation requests. With the particular administrative pressure of a surveillance apparatus that had just lost its cover story in the section of the corridor she managed. She knew what it looked like. She had watched it work on other positions over the years. A quiet escalation. A series of requests she couldn't honor without compromising the network. Eventually the position became untenable.

She was not there yet. She had some time.

She thought about what Adaeze had said, in that last conversation in September 2125 — not said, written, in the sealed letter Naila had held for seven months and delivered on the highway. The letter that had repaid the debt. But in the kitchen, drinking tea, before the letter, Adaeze had said things that were not the debt.

She had said: *Whatever you're going to have to give up when this is done, you'll know it was worth it. Not at the moment. Some time after.*

Naila looked at the morning outside. The maintenance bots working on the resurfacing. The person in the forecourt checking their planting. The freight carrier moving east, carrying goods between places that didn't appear on the Compact's maps.

Worth it was not the word she would use.

Necessary was closer.

She went to make tea for the morning's residents. Salt was still open. She was still its keeper. The position would change. She would adapt.

That was the work.

* * *

The Territorial Archive was on the south side of the administrative district — a pre-Compact building that had been built as a records repository and had survived the transition because someone, at the right moment, had made the case that records of what a place had been before it became something else were worth keeping. Perun was in the main reading room, not in the stacks. He was at the work table nearest the east window, with a document in front of him that she recognized as a pre-Compact protocol specification — the language dated it, in the way technical documents dated themselves by their formatting conventions.

He looked up when she came in.

He did not look surprised. He had the quality, she had come to understand, of a person who had spent a very long time getting ready for something without knowing when it would happen, and who had therefore arranged himself into a state of permanent readiness that looked like calm.

"Kirin," he said.

"Perun."

She sat across from him. She set both keys on the table: the card and the disc, together. Both in the continuous warmth that the joint handshake had left them in since the plateau. They were warmer together than apart. She had noticed this since the walk back and had not found a way to describe it that wasn't reductive.

He looked at them for a long time.

"Both," he said.

"Both." She watched him look. "The disc is Nnedi Eze's founding appointment, held by Rua per Nnedi's instruction, passed to me before departure. The card is Adaeze's interior appointment, now held by me as bearer. They completed a joint authentication handshake on March 4, 2126, on the plateau. The protocol moved from registered-dormant to active."

He touched the disc, very lightly, with the attention of a person who understood that handling was part of the record. He set it beside the card.

"Forty-seven years," he said. Not to her specifically.

"The last time both keys were together." She paused. "1978. The sector twelve archive isn't certain of the exact month."

He nodded. "I have been reviewing the protocol specification for the formal filing." He indicated the document in front of him. "There are requirements I have been working through for twenty-three years. The witness testimony. The authentication record. The infrastructure documentation supporting the claim's viability." He looked at her. "I believe the formatted survey from the sector twelve archive is complete. A relay came in this morning — the steward confirmed the final section yesterday afternoon. It will arrive here by end of day."

She looked at him. "You received a relay this morning."

"Through the Territorial Archive's pre-Compact document exchange. We maintain a direct relay connection with three outside archive nodes — one of which is the sector twelve steward's. It predates the Compact. It's in the building's original infrastructure." He held her eyes. "Nobody has thought to shut it down. Because nobody has thought to look for it."

She absorbed this.

"The third token," she said.

He reached into his desk drawer and brought out a tray — archival foam, the preservation quality — and on it: a key that was slightly different from the two on the table. Flatter, longer, the etching pattern running horizontally. Hasse's description, exactly. Forty-three years in this building.

"A founding precursor," he said. "No appointment. The prototype — the first physical attempt at the two-witness structure before the credential logic was settled. It doesn't carry an appointment and isn't needed for the filing." He looked at it. "But it belongs to the record. In 2083 someone brought it here and didn't come back for it. I have been holding it as part of the documentation."

"The person who left it," she said. "What happened to them."

"I don't know." He put the tray back in the drawer. "I've thought about it many times over forty-three years. I think they encountered the management apparatus in one form or another and were stopped. I think that apparatus has been running in some form since at least 2083, which means it predates whoever is currently running it." He paused. "Someone decided, early in the Compact's formation, that the Outside's existence should be managed rather than acknowledged. That decision was institutionalized. The institution outlasted the original decision-makers. The person running it now may not know where the original decision came from."

"He knows what he's been doing," she said.

"Yes." Perun looked at the two keys on the table. "What he's been doing is not the same thing as what was originally decided. These things drift." He paused. "What are you asking me."

She looked at the keys. Both warm. Both settled.

"I want to initiate the formal Rootstock Protocol filing," she said. "Under the two witness appointments. Interior appointment: the card, Adaeze Okonkwo's appointment, held by me as bearer. Founding appointment: the disc, Nnedi Eze's appointment, designated for filing in accordance with the plateau council's authorization." She paused. "The filing requires a qualified archival institution as the receiving party. I believe the Territorial Archive qualifies."

Perun looked at her.

"The Territorial Archive has been a pre-Compact records repository since 1982," he said. "Under the Compact's charter documents, all pre-existing archival institutions maintaining pre-Compact documentation retain their protocol-receiving authority for any filing system that predates the Compact's founding." He folded his hands. "I have been technically qualified to receive this filing for thirty-six years."

"I know," she said. "That's why I'm here."

He looked at the keys on the table. He looked at the protocol specification. He looked at the relay printout from the sector twelve steward.

"The formatted survey will arrive by end of day," he said. "The witness testimony needs to be recorded — I'll have the equipment in the west room. The authentication must be performed with both keys present and active, which they are." He paused. "I can have the filing prepared by tomorrow morning. It routes to the ASC's pre-Compact protocol office, which is staffed by one person and processes approximately four filings per decade." Another pause. "They will not be prepared for this."

"No," Kirin said. "They won't."

He held her eyes for a long moment.

"Your grandmother would have been glad," he said. "She was not a sentimental woman. But she would have been glad."

Kirin looked at the keys. Their continuous warmth. Both of them together on the archive's work table.

"She would have been glad it worked," she said. "She didn't much care about being glad."

Perun made a sound that was not quite a laugh and was the closest she had heard him come to one.

"No," he said. "She wouldn't." He straightened the document on the table. "Come back at four o'clock. We'll do the testimony then."


* * *

The afternoon was the particular quality of April in Sable River — the spring light going to gold as it descended, the specific slant of it on the building faces along the administrative district's main run.

She walked north.

Adaeze's apartment building was the same building it had always been. Pre-Compact original construction, maintained rather than renovated, the bones kept. The lock had not been changed since February.

She went up.

The apartment was the same. The window box herbs watered, the kitchen wiped down, the card from the building association on the counter. The room in its decades-old arrangement.

She sat in the reading chair.

The reading chair was the chair Adaeze had sat in every evening for as long as Kirin could remember. It had a particular quality of presence — a piece of furniture that has accommodated one person's weight for thirty-five years takes the shape of that person. She sat in it and understood for the first time why it had never been comfortable for her. It was not designed for other people.

Adaeze had done her Board filings here — the annual assessment reports, the compliance submissions, the maintenance documentation that Ardent had spent eighteen years reading without understanding. She had read those filings in the campus archive and had understood them as a logistics auditor's notes. Sitting in this chair, she understood them as the products of a person who sat here every evening and wrote down what she had seen and what it meant and what came next.

Thirty-five years. Every entry.

On the work table by the window: the specific mark where Adaeze's tea mug had sat every morning. Not a stain — Adaeze didn't leave stains — but a slight difference in the surface finish, the long history of the same object in the same place. The work table that had seen the Tamsat letters, the photographs, the log, the key in its box, all of it laid out and examined over decades of mornings.

She sat in the chair and looked at the garden outside.

The woman from the second floor was there, in the last of the afternoon light, doing what the late afternoon light made possible in the spring beds. Kirin did not know her name. The woman had been tending this garden for as long as Kirin could remember — since before Adaeze moved in, probably. A person who tended a garden not because it was hers but because it was there and needed tending.

She sat in the chair for a long time.

Then she went to the work table and sat in the work chair. She opened the drawer on the left side — the drawer where Adaeze had kept the blank paper she used for working notes. The drawer was empty except for one sheet, folded in half, with Kirin's name on the outside.

She had not found this in February. She had been sorting, not searching. She had not opened every drawer.

She unfolded it.

The handwriting was Adaeze's — the compressed, efficient hand of someone who had been writing quickly for a long time. Not a letter. Two sentences.

*There is nothing else hidden. You have found all of it. — A.*

She read it twice.

She folded it back up and put it in her jacket pocket with the two keys.

She went out of the apartment. She locked the door carefully behind her. On the stairs she passed the woman from the second floor, coming in from the garden with soil on her knees.

"She was good to have in the building," the woman said. Not the opener to a conversation — a fact, offered, with nowhere in particular to go.

"She was," Kirin said.

The woman went up. Kirin went down and out into the afternoon.

* * *

Tomas arrived at seven o'clock, which was exactly when he had said he would. He had been doing this since he was fifteen. He said it was respect for other people's planning. She thought it was that he preferred to be early and stood outside the door for the last two minutes, which she had caught him doing at least four times over the years.

He had brought food. Not out of obligation — out of the specific social intelligence of someone who knew that a person who had been doing a great deal all day would have forgotten to eat. Which she had. She made tea while he organized it on the kitchen counter with the focused efficiency of someone laying out tools.

"How did it go," he said.

"Brigid initiated a formal assessment under the emergency infrastructure protocol. The sector six junction failure gives her the standing. The documentation scope extends to all eleven designations." She poured the tea. "She contacted the Commons Council's emergency infrastructure division this morning."

He turned from the counter. "Is that enough."

She held the mug. "I don't know. The growing season starts in six weeks. The assessment runs three to five days. Whether the response chain moves fast enough after that — " She paused. "I don't know."

He carried the food to the table and sat. She sat across from him. Outside the window: the residential district behind the markets in its evening state, the particular sound of Sable River settling into its night.

"Perun is filing the Rootstock Protocol submission tomorrow morning," she said. "Both keys, the witness testimony, the formatted infrastructure survey. It routes to the ASC's pre-Compact protocol office."

Tomas looked at her with the specific look of a person processing the full weight of a sentence. "And the ASC's response time."

"Unknown." She held the mug. "The person running the pre-Compact protocol office has processed four filings in a decade." She paused. "But it's a formal filing. It's on record. The Compact will be structurally obligated to process it."

He sat with this. He ate, not hurriedly, with the quality of someone who was going to take the time a meal deserved. She had always liked this about him. He did not rush things that shouldn't be rushed.

"Your compliance flag," he said.

"Still on my account. Brigid knows about it — she used it to get my materials assessment into the emergency review. I don't know what happens to it when the assessment process runs." She paused. "The flag was applied by someone who ran out of ways to stop the documentation without the flag itself becoming a record of what he was stopping. So it's already on record that someone tried."

He nodded.

They ate. The evening outside. The city doing what it did in April, as it had done in all the Aprils before this one.

"My mother's corridor transit," he said, at some point in the middle of the meal. "She went east in 2094 and came back in eleven days looking at things from the inside out. She never said anything about it. She just — lived differently after. Quietly differently." He looked at his plate. "I've been thinking about why she went and what she might have seen." He looked up. "Was the Outside already — was it already what you found."

"It's been building since at least the 1970s," she said. "The plateau settlement was founded in 2067. The transit network was active by the 1990s. In 2094 your mother would have found communities, water management, relay networks. Less than now. The same shape."

He sat with this.

"She came back," he said. "She didn't stay."

"A lot of people come back. It's not that they don't care. It's that their life is here."

He looked at the window. "Is that what you're going to do."

She held the mug.

She had been asking herself this since the plateau — since the council meeting, since she had said she would think about it and been given three days and had accepted the role at the end of them. She had accepted the role of witness, which was not the same as a permanent post in a specific place. Adaeze had been the Compact interior witness for thirty-five years and had spent those years in Sable River in a flat not so different from this one.

"I'm going to be here," she said. "And I'm going to be the person who knows what I know." She looked at the window. "Those are the same job."

He nodded. Not satisfied, exactly — he was not a person who was easily satisfied — but accepting. The specific quality of someone who has asked the most important question and received an honest answer and understands that honest answers are not always complete ones.

They finished eating. He helped with the washing up in the way he had always helped, efficiently and in the specific order they had developed over years of each other's tables. They did not narrate the washing up.

When he was putting on his jacket to go, he said: "My mother used to hum something when she was working. I asked her once what it was and she said she didn't know where she'd learned it." He looked at his jacket's collar. "A song. I recognized it when you described the one your grandmother used to sing."

Kirin looked at him.

"The road is long," she said, "but there is water at the end of it."

"Yes." He stood in her doorway. "I was eleven. She came back from eleven days away and she started humming it and she never stopped." He paused. "I hadn't thought about it in twenty years until you described it in February. And then I couldn't stop."

He went out. She heard his footsteps on the stairs, his particular rhythm, the quality of someone who knew where he was going.

On the plateau, in the drainage work along the western slope, Rua received a relay from the second way-station at half past three.

She was up to her knees in the temporary bypass channel, directing two of the field crew in the secondary diversion while the main replacement housing was being machined at the settlement's workshop. The bypass was holding. It had been holding for five days and would hold for ten more. She had measured it carefully. She knew what a holding bypass looked like.

The relay was from Ade. Short, as relays were. *Filing initiated. Both keys. Formal record tomorrow.*

She read it standing in the drainage channel.

She handed it to Eme, who had come down from the slope to check on the diversion and was standing at the channel's edge. Eme read it and handed it back.

"That's it," Eme said.

"That's the filing," Rua said. "Whether it's it — " She folded the relay and put it in her vest pocket. "That depends on what happens next."

She went back to directing the crew.

The western slope caught the late afternoon light differently from the basin — the angle was shallower up here, the light cooler. She had worked this slope for nine years, since the infrastructure survey first flagged the sector six housing as approaching threshold. Nine years of watching a deterioration that the monitoring data reported clearly and the response system ignored.

The growing season began in six weeks.

The replacement housing would be ready in three. They would have it installed and running in five. One week of margin.

She worked steadily. The channel held. The late light moved in the direction late light moved, which was down.

* * *

She made Adaeze's tea at ten o'clock.

Not the tea she made herself — the particular blend Adaeze had kept in a tin above the stove, a mixture she had made up herself from market herbs and Guild distribution. Kirin had taken the tin from Adaeze's kitchen in February, the same week she had taken the box. It had been on her own shelf since, untouched.

She took down the tin and made the tea in Adaeze's way, which was different from her own, which was less patient. Adaeze had let it steep for four minutes and thirty seconds — not because four minutes was insufficient and five minutes was too much, but because four minutes and thirty seconds was what the tea required when you were making it right. Kirin had always thought this was fussiness. She understood now that it was precision. The two things were not the same.

She stood at the kitchen window while the tea steeped.

Outside: Sable River in April, in the evening, in the specific quality that had. Calibrated. Not loud. The Guild district's lights at the end of the cross-street. The commons garden at the block corner, the spring beds in. The maintenance relay tower at its regular interval, its beacon marking the eastern approach.

She had grown up here. She had walked these streets since she could walk. She had learned, seven years into her life, what it meant to come home to a place and find someone there who was glad you had come, and she had not had that since January, and she was not going to have it again.

She had Tomas. She had Hasse. She had Berin and Dez and Brigid and, at distance, Rua and Ade and Naila and Perun, all of them doing whatever they were going to do in the weeks and months ahead. She had two keys in her jacket pocket that had been warm since March. She had a note in her jacket that said *you have found all of it* in handwriting she had been looking at her whole life.

The kettle hummed.

Four minutes and thirty seconds.

She poured.

Outside: the cross-street. A woman walking east with a bag, her pace unhurried. Two Guild apprentices going north toward the residential buildings where the workshop sounds carried late. A delivery bot finishing its last run.

And in the building across the way — the grey masonry building that housed mixed residential and small workshops on the upper floors, that she had been looking at across this cross-street for three years — a light came on in the third-floor corner office. She had seen it before, many evenings, the pattern of someone who worked late in a space that looked out on this block.

And then the door — not the building's street door but the office door, visible through the window in the brief configuration of someone who had made a decision and was getting up to act on it — opened.

She could not see who it was. She could see the door.

She heard, at the distance of the cross-street and through her kitchen window, the particular sound of a door opening in a building where someone had decided what to do and was getting up to do it.

She held her tea.

The city outside was the city. It had been doing what it did before she was born and would do it after. It had been built for more people than lived in it. It had room.

The door was open.

She drank her tea.
